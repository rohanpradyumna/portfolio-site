The human mind is strangely creative.

On a random Sunday, you can be sitting on a bench, staring into nothing, and your brain is quietly building entire worlds.

Possibilities. Alternate realities. Half-formed ideas. Strange connections. Memories turning into patterns. Problems turning into products. Feelings turning into stories.

Most of us are more creative than we give ourselves credit for. The problem is not that we do not think. The problem is that very few people are able to take what is happening inside their head and turn it into something tangible.

Some people turn thoughts into words and become storytellers.

Some turn thoughts into scenes and become directors.

Some turn thoughts into books and become authors.

Some turn thoughts into products and become builders.

For a long time, that gap between thought and output was very difficult for me.

I have always had a tough time converting my thoughts into clean, processed language. Dyslexia and ADHD made writing feel like a heavy lift. I could feel the idea clearly in my head, but the moment I tried to put it into structured text, something would break. The thought would lose its shape. The emotion would disappear. The clarity I had in my mind would not survive the process of typing it out.

So for years, I had ideas that felt real to me but never became fully real in the world.

Then AI changed something very fundamental.

Tools like Wispr Flow removed a barrier I had lived with for most of my life. I no longer had to depend only on typing speed, spelling, sentence structure, or the traditional mechanics of writing to express what I was thinking. I could speak in my raw, unfiltered way and feed that thinking into an intelligence that could help me shape it into text, code, design, strategy, or product.

That is not a small thing.

For someone like me, AI did not replace creativity. It gave my creativity a way out.

The website I have been building, the one people have recently been complimenting me on, is a direct result of this barrier coming down. It is still a work in progress, and it will probably continue to be a work in progress, but it is one of the first times I have felt that something in my head could become something real without getting completely lost in translation.

To be clear, I did not write every line of code myself.

But the original thought was mine.

The direction was mine. The feeling was mine. The taste was mine. The decisions were mine. The version of me that I wanted the website to communicate was mine.

And the thing that made me happiest was not just that people liked it. It was that people said it felt like me.

That is the part I keep thinking about.

Because when I look at a lot of AI-generated portfolio websites now, many of them look strangely similar. Same sections. Same layouts. Same polished copy. Same animations. Same structure. Same feeling of something that was generated from a prompt and accepted too quickly.

Of course, some similarity is expected. A portfolio website has a job to do. It has to show who you are, what you have built, what you have worked on, and how someone can reach you.

But in a personal portfolio, sameness becomes a problem when the site stops carrying the person behind it.

When someone says, "I just gave Claude my resume and LinkedIn and asked it to build my portfolio," I understand the appeal. It is fast. It works. It gives you an output.

But does the output convey you?

That is the question I think we are not asking enough.

Because AI can give you a portfolio website. It can give you a LinkedIn post. It can give you a pitch deck. It can give you a landing page. It can give you a strategy document. It can give you almost any artifact you ask for.

But if you do not bring an original thought into the process, the output will not carry one.

It will simply look complete.

And that is the trap.

AI has made it easier than ever to create things, but it has also made it easier than ever to reveal that you did not really think about them.

That is what I find fascinating. AI does not always hide the lack of thought. In many cases, it exposes it.

The generic output. The over-polished writing. The same startup frameworks. The same "authentic" LinkedIn voice. The same product pages. The same personal brands. The same hooks. The same structure.

You can tell when someone used AI to express a thought.

And you can tell when someone used AI to avoid having one.

For full disclosure, this article was dictated by me and shaped with the help of AI, which is exactly the distinction I am trying to make.

The question is not whether AI was used.

The question is what role AI played.

Was it the author of the thought?

Or was it the engine that helped convert the thought into something real?

That difference matters.

I think the people who will be in demand over the next few years are not simply the people who use AI the most. Everyone will use AI. That will not be special.

The people who will stand out are the ones who know how to control AI instead of letting AI control the direction. The ones who have taste. The ones who know what they want before they ask for it. The ones who can look at a generated output and say, "This is clean, but this is not me."

That is the new skill.

Not prompting alone.

Judgment.

Taste.

Point of view.

Original thought.

AI can be an engine. It can be a machine. It can be a translator between your mind and the world. It can help convert a raw thought into a real product faster than ever before.

But it cannot decide what is worth saying for you.

It cannot live your life for you.

It cannot build your taste for you.

It cannot give you the courage to have an opinion.

So maybe the real advice is simple.

Go back to reading books.

Go back to watching great movies.

Go back to poetry.

Go back to travel.

Go back to conversations that make you uncomfortable.

Go back to boredom.

Go back to noticing things.

Build taste. Build original thought. Build the courage to expose that thought to the world. Then use AI to sharpen it, scale it, and turn it into something real.

Maybe I am wrong.

But I would rather put out an imperfect opinion that is actually mine than publish a polished article that says nothing.

Because in a world where everyone can generate output, the real advantage is not output anymore.

The real advantage is having something original to say.
